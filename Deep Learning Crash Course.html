Warm-up · W.1

AI, Machine Learning and Deep Learning are three circles, one inside the other

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Any machine that does something we would call "smart". This includes old programs where humans typed every rule by hand, such as a chess program from the 1990s.

Machine Learning (ML)

The machine learns the rules by itself from examples, instead of a human writing them. Show it thousands of past exam records and it learns to predict marks.

Deep Learning (DL)

A kind of ML that uses neural networks with many layers. It can learn straight from raw photos, sound or text, and it powers almost all modern AI.

Old way vs. new way: "Is this mango ripe?"

Traditional programming

Rules written by a human

a new mango→Answer

Breaks easily. What about a green variety that is ripe? Someone has to think of every rule.

Machine learning

Data
1000 mango photos+Answers
ripe / not ripe labels→Rules
learned by the computer

Give it examples with answers, and the computer finds the rule. This is called supervised learning.

You already use deep learning every day

Google Lens · face unlock on your phone · YouTube and Instagram recommendations · Google Translate (Hindi ↔ English) · voice typing · ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini · apps that spot disease on a crop leaf from a photo.

Warm-up · W.3

The slope tells you which way is downhill

Analogy

You are standing on a hill in thick fog, blindfolded, and want to reach the lowest point in the valley. You feel the ground with your foot. If it slopes down to the left, you step left. Small steps, again and again. That is gradient descent, the method almost every neural network uses to learn.

Try it
Step size (learning rate):
Loss L(w)–
Slope at w–
new w = w − (step size) × slope

Loss here is L(w) = ½(w − 3)² + 1. Its lowest point is at w = 3.

What is a slope (derivative)?

It answers: "If I nudge w a tiny bit, how much does the loss change?" Positive slope means loss goes up when w goes right, so we move left. The minus sign in the update rule does exactly that.

Many knobs

A real network has millions of knobs. We find the slope for each knob separately (this is called a partial derivative). The full list of these slopes is called the gradient, written ∇L.

Watch out

Try step size 0.1 (slow but safe), 1.9 (bounces from side to side) and 2.1 (jumps further away every step: the training "explodes"). Picking the learning rate is one of the most important choices in deep learning.

Warm-up · W.4

The special number e ≈ 2.718, and its reverse, ln

You will see e inside sigmoid, softmax and the loss function. Here is where it comes from and the four facts you need.

A bank story

You put ₹1 in a bank that gives 100% interest per year. If the bank adds interest more and more often, how much do you get after one year?

Money after 1 year–

The answer never passes ₹2.71828…, no matter how often. That limit is the number e.

Graph of eˣ

Four facts to remember

  • eˣ is always positive, even when x is negative. Softmax uses this to turn any score into a positive number.
  • e⁰ = 1. Big x gives a huge number. Very negative x gives almost 0.
  • e⁻ˣ = 1 / eˣ. For example e⁻¹ = 1/2.718 ≈ 0.368.
  • ln (natural log) undoes e: ln(e²) = 2, ln(1) = 0, and ln of a number between 0 and 1 is negative: ln(0.7) ≈ −0.357. This is why the loss has a minus sign in front.

To a computer, a photo is just a grid of numbers

The one calculation inside every neural network: the dot product

Take two lists of the same length, multiply them pair by pair, then add everything up. Press Next step to see it.

Words you will hear

A list of numbers is a vector. A grid of numbers is a matrix. A block with even more directions (like a colour image: height × width × 3) is a tensor. That is where the name Tensor-flow comes from. PyTorch also calls its data "tensors".

History · H.1

It started with a question: can we copy how the brain works?

Your brain has about 86 billion nerve cells called neurons. Each one connects to thousands of others. A neuron collects signals, and if the total is strong enough, it "fires" and passes a signal on.

Dendrites → inputs

Branches that receive signals from other neurons. In the artificial neuron: the inputs x₁, x₂, x₃.

Synapse strength → weights

Some connections are strong, some weak. Learning in the brain changes these strengths. In the artificial neuron: the weights w.

Cell body → sum + activation

Adds up incoming signals and decides whether to fire. In the artificial neuron: z = w·x + b, then f(z).

Axon → output

Carries the signal to the next neurons. In the artificial neuron: the output, which becomes the input of the next layer.

Be careful with this analogy

Artificial neurons are only inspired by real ones. Real neurons are far more complex (they fire in spikes, use chemicals, and change over time). A neural network is really just maths: multiply, add, squash. Repeated many times.

History · H.3

Why did it suddenly work after 2012? Three ingredients arrived together.

Ingredient 1

Data

The internet and phone cameras created billions of photos and text. ImageNet (2009) labelled about 14 million images by hand. Neural networks need a lot of examples.

Compute

Hour 1 · 1.1

A single neuron: multiply, add, squash

Let's turn a real decision into a neuron. "Should I go to the cricket match today?" Three facts go in, a probability comes out.

Try itStarting values are the worked example from the notes: x = [1, 0, 1], w = [0.5, −0.3, 0.8], b = 0.1
Activation:
Weight

How much an input matters. A negative weight means the input pushes against the decision (an exam tomorrow makes you less likely to go).

Bias

How much you want to go before looking at any facts. It shifts the whole decision up or down.

Activation f

Squashes the total into a useful range. Sigmoid gives a probability between 0 and 1.

Connection to the ML course

Logistic regression is a classic ML method: it computes z = w·x + b and passes it through sigmoid to get a probability. That is exactly this neuron. So logistic regression is a single neuron, and a neural network is many of them connected in layers. Rosenblatt's 1958 perceptron used a hard step function (output exactly 0 or 1). Modern neurons use smooth functions, because we need a slope to learn (Hour 2).

Why ReLU beats sigmoid in hidden layers

Move the slider to z = 5 on sigmoid: the curve is almost flat and the slope is only 0.0066. A flat curve means a tiny slope, and a tiny slope means almost no learning. Its best slope anywhere is only 0.25 (at z = 0). ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for every positive z, so the learning signal does not shrink. ReLU is also cheap: just one comparison.

Extra detail

ReLU has its own weakness: for negative z the slope is 0, so a neuron that always receives negative input stops learning forever (a "dead ReLU"). Leaky ReLU fixes this with a small slope (0.01 here) on the left. Large language models usually use smooth relatives of ReLU such as GELU.

Hour 1 · 1.2 continued

Softmax turns raw scores into probabilities that add up to 1

The last layer gives one raw score per class (called logits). Softmax converts them into probabilities. Example from the notes: is the photo a Cat, Dog or Bird?

Try it
Why use ez?

It turns every score positive (even negative ones), keeps the order (bigger score → bigger probability), and makes the biggest score stand out. Dividing by the sum makes everything add up to exactly 1.

Two subtle facts

1. Adding the same number to every score changes nothing. Try the button. Only the differences between scores matter. 2. Doubling every score makes the model more confident (the winner gets a bigger share), even though the order stays the same.

Sigmoid or softmax?

Two classes (spam / not spam): one output neuron with sigmoid. Three or more classes, where exactly one is correct: one neuron per class with softmax.

What makes it "deep"?

"Deep" learning just means more than one hidden layer. There is no strict cut-off. Fun fact: the network on the right with two hidden layers is often called a "3-layer network", because people do not count the input layer (it does no computation).

Every line is a weight

Each line in the diagram is one weight, and each non-input neuron has one bias. Parameters in one layer = (inputs × neurons) + neurons. Fully-connected layers like these are also called dense layers, or nn.Linear in PyTorch.

Forward propagation: push numbers from input to output

A tiny network: 2 inputs → 2 hidden neurons (ReLU) → 1 output (sigmoid). Press Next step (or →) to compute it one neuron at a time.

change the inputs and step through again
How code does this (matrix form)

All hidden neurons are computed at once with a matrix: h = ReLU(W₁·x + b₁), where W₁ = [[0.5, −0.2], [0.3, 0.4]] and b₁ = [0.1, −0.05]. Then ŷ = σ(W₂·h + b₂). GPUs are very fast at exactly this kind of matrix multiplication, and that is why deep learning runs on GPUs.

Remember

Every time ChatGPT writes a word, or your phone recognises your face, it does this same forward pass. Only the network is far bigger.

Hour 2 · 2.1

The loss function gives one number: how wrong was the network?

For predicting a number: MSE

Mean Squared Error = average of (true − predicted)². Used for marks, prices, temperature. You met it in W.2.

For choosing a class: Cross-Entropy

Loss = − Σ yᵢ · ln(pᵢ)

y is the correct answer written as one-hot: a list with 1 for the true class and 0 elsewhere. Cat = [1, 0, 0].

Notes exampleTrue label: Cat → y = [1, 0, 0]. Model says p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1].
Loss = −[ 1 × ln(0.7) + 0 × ln(0.2) + 0 × ln(0.1) ]
     = −ln(0.7) = −(−0.357) = 0.357
The zeros in y wipe out every term except the true class. Only "how much probability did you give the right answer?" matters.
Try it
Loss = −ln(p)–

Analogy

A student who says "I'm 99% sure the answer is B" and is wrong deserves to lose more marks than one who says "maybe B". Cross-entropy works like that: confident and wrong is punished very heavily (p = 0.05 gives loss ≈ 3.0), while confident and right costs almost nothing (p = 0.99 gives loss ≈ 0.01).

Extra detail

For yes/no problems the formula becomes binary cross-entropy: −[y·ln(p) + (1 − y)·ln(1 − p)]. In PyTorch, nn.CrossEntropyLoss expects the raw scores (logits) and applies softmax inside by itself. That is why the model in Hour 4 has no softmax layer at the end.

Hour 2 · before backprop

The chain rule: when things are connected, multiply the rates

Gears

A neural network is a chain of simple steps: weight → weighted sum → activation → next layer → … → loss. To learn how the loss changes when one weight changes, we multiply the small rates along the chain.

A tiny maths example
y = (3x + 1)². Let u = 3x + 1, so y = u². At x = 1: u = 4, y = 16.
How fast y changes with u: dy/du = 2u = 8
How fast u changes with x: du/dx = 3
Chain rule: dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx = 8 × 3 = 24
Check by nudging x to 1.001: y = (4.003)² = 16.024009. Change ≈ 0.024 for a 0.001 nudge → 0.024 / 0.001 ≈ 24 ✓

Analogy

Your team loses a cricket match by 30 runs. The coach works backward: how much did the last over cost? How much of that was the bowler, how much the fielding? Each player gets blame in proportion to their effect on the result, and practises to fix exactly that. Backprop does this for every weight.

Hour 2 · 2.3

Ways of walking downhill: batch, SGD, mini-batch, momentum, Adam

Race

The ellipses are contour lines of a loss surface shaped like a long narrow valley, a shape that is common in real networks. The ★ is the lowest point. Noise in SGD and mini-batch is simulated.

Batch GD

Uses all training examples for each step. Accurate direction, but slow per step and needs lots of memory.

Stochastic GD (SGD)

Uses one example per step. Very fast steps, but the direction is noisy and jumpy.

Mini-batch GD

Uses a small group, e.g. 32 or 64 examples. The middle ground almost everyone uses. Fits GPUs well.

Three words that confuse everyone

Batch size = examples per step. Iteration = one update step. Epoch = one full pass over all training data. Fashion-MNIST has 60,000 training images; with batch size 64 that is 60,000 ÷ 64 = 937.5, so 938 iterations per epoch (the last batch has only 32 images).

Adam in one line

Adam = momentum + an automatic step size for every weight. Weights whose gradients are usually large get smaller steps, and rarely-updated weights get bigger ones. Hour 2 · 2.4

Multiply many small numbers and you get almost nothing

The gradient for an early layer is a product of one factor per layer (activation slope × weight). If each factor is below 1, the product shrinks fast. If each is above 1, it blows up.

Try it

Verdict–

Simplified model: every layer contributes the same factor. Bars use a log scale: each grid line is 10× smaller than the one above.

Vanishing (the notes example)

10 sigmoid layers: 0.2510 ≈ 0.00000095. The first layers get almost no learning signal and stay nearly random. This is the main reason deep networks were so hard to train before about 2010.

Exploding

Set ReLU and weight size 2: each layer doubles the gradient. After 30 layers it is about a billion times bigger, the update steps become huge, and the numbers turn into "NaN" (not a number).

The fixes (all covered in this course)

  • ReLU activation, whose slope is 1 (1.2)
  • Careful initial weights (Xavier / He) so the factor starts close to 1 (2.6)
  • Batch normalisation keeps values in a healthy range (2.5)
  • Skip connections give the gradient a shortcut past layers (ResNet, 3.5)
  • LSTM gates for long sequences (4.2)
  • Gradient clipping: if the gradient is too large, scale it down to a maximum size. Fixes exploding.

Subtle details about dropout

Dropout is used only during training. At test time every neuron is on (PyTorch switches with model.train() and model.eval()). So that the total signal stays the same size, the surviving neurons are multiplied by 1/(1 − p) during training: with p = 0.5 they are doubled. This is called inverted dropout. Other regularisers: data augmentation (flip, rotate or crop training images to make "new" examples) and weight decay (L2: gently push all weights toward 0).

Hour 2 · 2.5 continued

Batch normalisation keeps the numbers in a healthy range

For each neuron, take its values across the current mini-batch, shift them to mean 0 and scale them to spread 1. Then let the network re-scale them with two learnable numbers, γ (scale) and β (shift).

One neuron, a mini-batch of 4 images
x̂ = (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε)     y = γ·x̂ + β
Subtle details

ε is a tiny number (like 0.00001) so we never divide by zero. During training μ and σ come from the current mini-batch; at test time the network uses running averages saved during training. Why learn γ and β? So the network can undo the normalisation if that works better. With γ = σ and β = μ, you get the original numbers back. Transformers use a close cousin called Layer Normalisation, which normalises across the neurons of one example instead of across the batch.

Hour 2 · 2.6

If all neurons start the same, they stay the same forever

A real experiment running in your browser. A 2 → 2 → 1 network learns XOR (output 1 when exactly one input is 1). We only change how the weights start.

Experiment

The symmetry problem

Identical neurons compute identical outputs, so backprop gives them identical gradients, so they update identically. 100 identical neurons behave like 1 neuron. Random starting values break the symmetry.

How random? Xavier and He

Too big → exploding. Too small → vanishing. The fix: scale the randomness by the number of inputs n to the layer. Xavier (for sigmoid/tanh): spread ≈ √(1/n). He (for ReLU): spread ≈ √(2/n). For a layer with 784 inputs, He gives √(2/784) ≈ 0.05. PyTorch does a version of this automatically.

Idea 1 · Look locally

To spot an edge or an eye, you only need a small patch of nearby pixels, not the whole photo at once. So each neuron looks at a small window, like 3×3.

With the vertical-edge filter, big positive or negative numbers mean "there is a vertical edge here" (bright-to-dark or dark-to-bright). Near 0 means no vertical edge. Full output for the notes example: [[−2, 0, 2], [−3, −2, 2], [−3, −1, 2]].

Padding and stride

Padding adds a border of zeros so edge pixels get used as often as middle ones, and with P = 1 a 3×3 filter keeps the size the same ("same" padding: 5 → 5). Stride is how many cells the filter jumps each move; stride 2 roughly halves the output size.

Two subtle details

1. In a real CNN the filter numbers are not chosen by a human. They start random and are learned by backprop, just like any weight. The network discovers edge filters by itself. 2. On a colour image a 3×3 filter is really 3×3×3: it covers all 3 colour channels at once and adds everything into one number. A layer with 16 filters produces 16 feature maps (16 "channels").

Key facts

Pooling has no learnable weights. A 2×2 window with stride 2 cuts width and height in half, so the map has 4× fewer numbers, making the next layers cheaper.

Winning entry each year. Top-5 error = the right answer was not in the model's 5 best guesses. Human ≈ 5.1% (one trained person, measured in 2014).

ResNet's skip connection

A normal block computes F(x). A residual block computes F(x) + x: the input skips around and is added back.

The problem it solved: before ResNet, a plain 56-layer network got worse than a 20-layer one, even on the training data (the "degradation problem"). Extra layers should at least be able to copy their input, but in practice they could not learn to.

Why the shortcut helps: a block only has to learn the small change F(x) to make. And during backprop the "+ x" path passes the gradient straight through, multiplied by 1, so it does not vanish across 100+ layers. Transformers (Hour 4) use the same trick in every layer.

LSTM: a memory conveyor belt with three gates

Analogy

Think of a notebook you carry through a lecture. At each moment you decide: erase some old notes (forget gate), write some new notes (input gate), and say out loud part of what's written (output gate). A gate is a number from 0 (fully closed) to 1 (fully open), produced by a sigmoid.

GRU

The Gated Recurrent Unit (2014) is a simpler version with two gates (update and reset) and no separate cell state. Fewer parameters, faster to train, and often about as good as an LSTM.

Hour 4 · 4.3

Attention: let every word look directly at every other word

RNNs read one word at a time and squeeze everything into one memory. Attention lets each word ask: "which other words matter to me right now?", and look at them all at once.

Click a word

Illustrative weights, chosen by hand to show the idea. A trained model's attention heads show patterns like this.

Query, Key, Value: like a YouTube search

Query = what you type ("cricket highlights"). Keys = each video's title. Values = the videos themselves. Compare the query with every key to get a match score, turn scores into weights with softmax, then take a weighted blend of the values. In a Transformer, every word makes its own query, key and value vectors with learned weights.

Notes example · press Next step
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d ) · V
Subtle details

Why divide by √d? d is the length of the key vectors (64 in the original Transformer). Dot products of long vectors get large, which pushes softmax into giving almost all weight to one word, where its slope is nearly 0 (vanishing gradients again). Dividing by √d keeps the scores moderate. Multi-head attention runs several attentions side by side (8 heads in the original paper), so one head can track grammar while another tracks who "it" refers to.

Hour 4 · 4.3 continued

The Transformer: attention stacked many times, and how ChatGPT uses it

Speed: RNN vs Transformer

An RNN must finish word 1 before starting word 2. A Transformer processes all words in parallel, which fits GPUs perfectly. That is why it can be trained on internet-scale text.

Inside one Transformer block

You already know every part

Self-attention (4.3) · feed-forward layer = the dense network from Hour 1 · Add = ResNet skip connection (3.5) · Norm = layer normalisation, cousin of batch norm (2.5). Stack this block many times: GPT-3 uses 96 blocks.

Positional encoding

Attention by itself ignores word order: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look the same. So a pattern of numbers that encodes each word's position is added to its vector before the first block.

Tokens

Text is split into tokens: whole words or pieces of words ("playing" → "play" + "ing"). Each token becomes a vector of numbers (an embedding), as in W.5.

How a chatbot writes: predict the next token

"The farmer watered the ____"

The model outputs a score for every token in its vocabulary, then softmax (1.2) turns them into probabilities, and one word is picked. Then it repeats with the new word added. Temperature divides the scores before softmax: low = safe and repetitive, high = creative and random. (Scores here are illustrative.)

BERT (Google, 2018)

Uses the encoder half: reads the whole sentence in both directions. Good for understanding: search, classification.

GPT (OpenAI, 2018 onward)

Uses the decoder half: only looks at earlier words and predicts the next one. Good for generating text. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are built on this idea, then further trained to follow instructions.

Hour 4 · 4.4

Transfer learning: reuse a network that already knows how to see

Analogy

If you can already ride a bicycle, learning a scooter takes a day, not months. Balance and steering transfer. A network trained on 1.2 million ImageNet photos already knows edges, textures and shapes. You only teach it your new classes.

Example: crop disease from 800 leaf photos

In PyTorch (modern torchvision)
from torchvision import models
import torch.nn as nn

model = models.resnet18(weights="DEFAULT")       # pretrained on ImageNet
for p in model.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False                        # freeze: no gradients, no updates
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, 5)      # new head for 5 classes (trainable)

Older tutorials (and the notes) write resnet18(pretrained=True). That argument is deprecated in torchvision 0.13 and later; weights="DEFAULT" is the current way.

Hour 4 · 4.6 and 4.7

Where deep learning struggles, and what to learn next

Data hunger

Training from scratch needs lots of labelled examples. Fix: transfer learning (4.4), data augmentation.

Compute cost

Big models need GPUs or TPUs and a lot of electricity. Even the small Hour 4 CNN is slow on a laptop CPU. Fix: Google Colab or Kaggle notebooks give free GPUs.

Hard to explain

A decision tree shows its exact rules. A network with millions of weights does not. Explaining why it decided something is an active research area (explainable AI).

Overfitting on small data

Millions of parameters can memorise a few thousand examples. Fix: dropout, augmentation, early stopping (2.5).

Bias in data

A model learns whatever is in its data, including unfairness. A face model trained mostly on one group of people works worse on others. Check your data.

Confidently wrong

Networks can give 99% confidence on nonsense, and chatbots can invent facts ("hallucinate"). Always verify important outputs.

One sentence to remember

A neural network is a big function with millions of knobs; we measure how wrong it is with a loss, use the chain rule to find which way to turn every knob, and repeat until it's good. Every model from 1986's backprop to today's chatbots follows this loop.
